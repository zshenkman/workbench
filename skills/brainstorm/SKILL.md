# Brainstorm

Develop the product idea before narrowing it into a specification. Help the user
understand what the product could be, why it might matter, what is actually
known, and which questions or possibilities deserve more serious design.

Treat the output as a working creative artifact. It may contain competing ideas,
rough sketches in words, unresolved tensions, surprising references, and
questions that are more valuable than premature answers.

## Keep the work exploratory and grounded

Let evidence, user intent, and real uncertainty determine the breadth of the
work. Do not measure a brainstorm by the number or novelty of ideas it contains.
A single well-grounded direction explored deeply can be more useful than several
manufactured alternatives.

Hold ideas lightly:

- distinguish confirmed facts, observations, interpretations, hypotheses,
  possibilities, constraints, and unknowns;
- do not invent users, needs, behaviors, constraints, or preferences to make the
  concept feel complete;
- use a hypothesis only when it helps expose what must be learned, and state the
  evidence that would support or disprove it;
- use working names freely and rename concepts as understanding improves;
- preserve contradictions when they reveal a real product choice;
- explore alternatives when they represent a genuine decision or unresolved
  tension;
- leave questions open when the evidence is not ready to support a decision.

Do not turn the brainstorm into a product specification. Avoid exhaustive
feature catalogs, permission matrices, acceptance criteria, exact routes,
complete error-state inventories, implementation architecture, delivery phases,
or completeness checklists. Capture those only when a detail is itself the
source of an interesting idea or a non-negotiable constraint.

## Use a loose canvas

Read `templates/brainstorm.md` as a menu, not a form. Copy it to the user's
requested location or to `docs/brainstorm.md` when a durable artifact would be
useful. Delete, rename, reorder, or ignore any section that does not help the
thinking. Start from a blank page when that better fits the prompt.

Write in the shape the material wants: narrative, bullets, concept cards,
questions, short scenarios, diagrams, rough page maps, or a mixture. Do not fill
space to make the output look complete.

## Find the spark

Begin with the smallest useful framing:

- What change in a person's life or work makes this idea interesting?
- Who might care most, and in what moment?
- What tension, frustration, desire, or new capability creates the opening?
- Which constraints are real, and which are inherited assumptions?
- What would make the result feel distinct rather than merely functional?

For an existing project, inspect enough product evidence to understand its
current promise, vocabulary, surfaces, users, and constraints. Do not inventory
the whole implementation. Treat existing behavior as material to question, not
as the boundary of the possible.

When context is missing, preserve the unknown instead of quietly filling it.
Continue with parts of the exploration that do not depend on the missing answer,
research what can be discovered, and ask the user a concise high-leverage
question when the answer would materially change the concept. If a provisional
hypothesis is useful, phrase conclusions conditionally and do not build a chain
of further claims on it.

## Roam the landscape

Explore comparable products and industry knowledge as evidence and creative
input. Unless the user excludes research or no useful comparison exists, select
references that illuminate the actual problem:

- direct products solving the same job;
- adjacent products serving a neighboring workflow;
- analogous experiences in a different industry when the analogy maps to a real
  user need or operating constraint;
- excellent examples of a relevant interaction, community, trust model,
  business model, or service pattern;
- platform conventions, cultural expectations, new technology, regulations, or
  shifts that change what is possible.

Prefer current first-party sources and direct product observation for factual
claims. Use credible commentary, research, and reviews to find user language,
friction, and unmet needs. Record links and observation dates for anything that
may change.

When the context supports it, a reference may suggest a pattern to:

- adopt because familiarity matters;
- adapt to a different user or context;
- invert to create a different experience;
- combine across categories;
- strip down to its essential value;
- avoid because the common pattern causes recurring friction.

Do not produce a generic competitor matrix or feature-parity list. Ask what each
reference teaches about the problem, users, constraints, conventions, or
available choices. Separate what was observed from what was inferred.

## Explore alternatives when they matter

Begin with the most grounded interpretation of the opportunity. Develop another
direction only when it helps examine a real fork, such as conflicting user
needs, a consequential product boundary, a different ownership model, or a
meaningfully different way to deliver the value. Do not create alternatives to
satisfy a quota.

When alternatives are warranted, make their underlying choice explicit. Useful
contrasts may include:

- tool versus guide;
- private workspace versus shared place;
- deliberate session versus ambient assistance;
- blank canvas versus strong opinion;
- expert power versus instant approachability;
- automation versus human agency;
- destination versus layer inside an existing workflow;
- broad platform versus one magical narrow job.

Use creative moves selectively when the current framing seems inherited,
contradictory, or unnecessarily narrow:

- **Remove the default:** What if the expected central feature did not exist?
- **Change the unit:** What if the product centered on moments, decisions,
  relationships, outcomes, or rituals instead of files and projects?
- **Check the actor:** Does evidence suggest that a novice, expert, team,
  customer, operator, or another actor experiences the problem differently?
- **Change the timescale:** What should happen in ten seconds, one day, or one
  year?
- **Test an extreme:** What does a fully automatic, radically manual, temporary,
  public, local, invisible, or single-action version reveal about the real
  value and tradeoffs?
- **Borrow a model:** What would a studio, game, concierge, marketplace,
  notebook, coach, or instrument suggest?
- **Start at the ending:** What memorable result should the user walk away with?

Treat these as questions, not instructions to invent a product around every
prompt. Stop generating alternatives when additional directions no longer teach
anything material. If one direction remains clearly strongest, deepen it.

## Make ideas tangible

Give the promising direction or live alternatives a little lived reality. Use
whichever artifacts make the concept easiest to understand:

- a one-sentence promise;
- a scene from a user's day;
- the first minute and the returning-user ritual;
- a rough journey or page-and-access-point constellation;
- the product's main object, action, or metaphor;
- a signature moment that users might describe to someone else;
- sample language, notifications, or interactions;
- a “this product says yes/no to” pair;
- a small Mermaid sketch when relationships or motion matter.

Keep these sketches suggestive. Do not resolve every state or edge case.

## Apply useful pressure

Critique ideas after they have room to develop. Ask:

- What user truth makes this compelling?
- What must be true for it to work?
- Where does it create new friction, exclusion, risk, or loss of agency?
- Which part is genuinely differentiated, and which part is familiar packaging?
- What becomes possible because of current technology or behavior?
- What should remain human?
- What is the smallest expression that preserves the magic?
- Which non-negotiable safety, accessibility, privacy, legal, or platform
  constraint changes the direction?

Treat feasibility evidence proportionally. Separate “impossible,” “expensive,”
“unknown,” and “unfamiliar,” but do not describe an idea as feasible without
support. Preserve a useful core when its first form is impractical.

## Synthesize without closing

End with orientation, not false certainty. Surface:

- the strongest understanding of the problem and why the idea fits it;
- the strongest ideas and the evidence, user intent, or reasoning behind them;
- compelling fragments worth combining when multiple directions remain live;
- the most important tensions or forks;
- unknowns and hypotheses that need research or a prototype;
- ideas intentionally set aside and what they taught;
- one or more promising directions for the user to react to;
- material that is ready to enter a later product specification.

Recommend a direction only when the evidence supports doing so. Present multiple
possibilities only when they remain genuinely live, and explain the question or
evidence that would distinguish them.

Report the brainstorm path when a file was created, research sources used,
promising directions, and the highest-value next question or experiment. Make
clear that the artifact is exploratory and should inform—not masquerade as—the
later specification.
